## 2. Creation of the McPeak Triangle Equation in 2012

### The Original Engineering Problem

**In 2012, Dearl McPeak investigated a fundamental question:**

**How can the phase of a traveling wave be measured at any point in space?**

The problem involved finding a practical way to determine the phase of a traveling wave by comparing it with a reference wave.

Traditional approaches to wave analysis frequently rely on time, frequency, phase measurements, and specialized instrumentation. McPeak sought a different approach that could use measurable wave magnitudes and geometric relationships.

His objective was to develop a method that could relate a wave's magnitude to its phase relative to a reference.

### The Development of the Geometric Concept

**McPeak applied an analogy involving:**

**1.** A ruler used to measure physical distance.

**2.** A reference sine wave used to establish a phase relationship.

**3.** A traveling wave whose phase could be determined relative to that reference.

After four days of exploring the problem, he recognized that **a right triangle could provide a useful geometric representation of the relationship between wave magnitude and phase.**

This insight led to the development of the McPeak Triangle concept.

The central idea was to represent wave magnitudes using the sides of a right triangle and use the angle of that triangle to represent phase.

McPeak's approach treats the wave magnitude as the triangle's magnitude rather than simply referring to it as a conventional hypotenuse.

He also established a reference position from which the wave's magnitude and phase relationship could be evaluated.

### The McPeak Triangle Equation

The basic equation associated with his work is:

**Δy=±Δθ⋅h⋅cos⁡(θ)**

**Where:**

**Δy** represents the change in the resultant or measured magnitude.

**Δθ** represents a change in phase angle.

**h** represents the wave magnitude.

**θ** represents the relevant phase angle.

The positive or negative sign represents the direction of change.

The equation uses the cosine relationship to describe how a change in phase can affect the measured magnitude.

McPeak's stated preference for cosine is based on the geometric arrangement of his triangle, in which the adjacent component is associated with the y-axis.

He also emphasizes that the equation is intended to help describe a wave's magnitude in relation to its phase, rather than to represent a simple loss of amplitude caused by distance or transmission.

### A Distinctive Feature of the Concept

**An important feature of McPeak's explanation is his distinction between:**

- A wave's magnitude relative to a reference.
- A wave's phase relative to that reference.
- The change in magnitude resulting from a change in phase.
- The physical loss of signal strength that can occur during transmission.

His stated objective is to use geometric relationships to examine wave behavior without making elapsed time the central variable in the basic equation.

## 3. Development of the McPeak Traveling Wave Equation

McPeak distinguishes between the **McPeak Triangle Equation** and **the McPeak Traveling Wave Equation**.

The Triangle Equation represents the basic geometric relationship.

The Traveling Wave Equation incorporates additional logic intended to account for the direction of phase movement, angular orientation, and the position of a wave within a complete 360-degree cycle.

### Determining the True Phase Angle

One of the developments associated with McPeak's work is a method for determining whether an angle should be interpreted within the first 180 degrees or within the remaining portion of the full cycle.

**His stated logic includes the relationship:**

$$
\theta =
\begin{cases}
360^\circ - \theta', & \text{if } \text{sign}(\Delta y) = \text{sign}(\Delta\theta), \\[6pt]
\theta', & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

**Here:**

- **θ′** represents the initially calculated angle.
- **θ** theta represents the angle after applying the directional rule.
- **Δy** represents the change in measured magnitude.
- **Δθ** represents the change in phase.
- **The sign comparison is used to determine the appropriate angular interpretation.**

McPeak has described this logic as a way to account for the direction in which a traveling wave progresses through its cycle.

His broader formulation also considers angular positions beyond 360 degrees.

These developments form part of his ongoing effort to document a complete framework for analyzing traveling-wave behavior.
